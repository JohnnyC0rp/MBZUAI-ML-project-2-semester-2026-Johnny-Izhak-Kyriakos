\section{Feature Engineering}

The best features were not generic transformations; they were domain features that translated banking intuition into measurable risk. We grouped the final engineered features into four families:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Affordability ratios}: loan share of income, monthly payment share, debt pressure, and asset coverage.
    \item \textbf{Delinquency severity}: weighted 30/60/90-day late-payment counts and their interactions with utilization and debt ratios.
    \item \textbf{Derogatory burden}: count and severity aggregates for charge-offs, collections, bankruptcies, and public records.
    \item \textbf{Semantic missingness}: borrower-context flags such as renter-without-property-value or owner-without-mortgage-balance.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:feature-stages} shows how the project score moved as we layered these ideas into the pipeline. The biggest jump came from moving beyond basic preprocessing into finance-aware ratios and controlled interaction terms. Later gains were smaller, but still meaningful enough to matter on the leaderboard.

The classification and regression tasks shared several strong signals, but the ranking was not identical. Figure~\ref{fig:feature-signal} shows that delinquency severity and utilization interactions dominate the class prediction task, while affordability and balance-sheet coverage become relatively more important for interest-rate prediction. That difference is exactly what we would expect in lending: default risk and pricing are related, but pricing also reacts to continuous affordability details.
